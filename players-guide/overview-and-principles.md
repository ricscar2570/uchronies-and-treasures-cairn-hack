# Overview & Principles

**Uchronies & Treasures** is a time-crime horror roleplaying game for one Warden and two to five players. Each player portrays an underpaid Temporal Division agent sent into places where incompatible histories overlap. The team enters to recover a person, relic, or piece of evidence. What follows them home is often more dangerous than what they were sent to retrieve.

The game is about competent people making consequential decisions under pressure. Temporal zones damage bodies and certainty. Institutions turn survival into debt. Every faction can offer protection, but none offers it without a claim. The central question is not simply whether the agents complete the mission. It is **what they are willing to owe, conceal, surrender, or become in order to return**.

> You do not need to memorize the rules before play. Learn what your agent can perceive, state what they are trying to achieve, and ask what the danger will cost. The system becomes most visible when a choice creates risk.

## What Happens at the Table

Play is a conversation supported by procedures.

1. The Warden describes the situation, including what is immediately apparent and what is pressing.
2. The players ask questions, examine details, and decide what their agents attempt.
3. The Warden explains what happens when the approach is safe and plausible.
4. When an action is possible, uncertain, dangerous, and consequential, the Warden calls for a save after making the danger legible.
5. The result stands. Time, resources, opponents, and the physical world continue from what actually happened.

A save is not required merely because an action is difficult. If an agent has the right tools, enough time, and relevant experience, the action may simply work. If an action is impossible, the Warden explains what prevents it. Dice enter play when the outcome is uncertain and a real danger can follow from the attempt. A failed save is allowed to mean that the stated objective is not achieved.

### A Brief Example

The agents need a sealed case from a hotel room that existed in 1956 and 2080 at the same time. The Warden describes a flickering corridor, a locked door, and a security team approaching from the modern stairwell.

Reyes says she wants to force the door. With unlimited time she could eventually break it, but the approaching guards make delay dangerous. The Warden explains that the attempt takes one exploration turn and that failure leaves the door closed when security reaches the corridor. Reyes makes a STR save. On a success, the door opens in time. On a failure, it remains shut; the turn passes, the guards arrive, and the team must retreat through the service hatch, surrender, bargain, or fight from the corridor.

Another player notices the 1956 service hatch and asks whether a former burglar would recognize the lock. The answer may reveal a safer route and remove the need for the save entirely. Questions and preparation do not decorate the roll. They change whether a roll is needed and what it can cost.

## Start With Essential Bookkeeping

For a first session, do **not** use every campaign ledger in the book. Start with character sheets, one mission record, the team campaign sheet, and one shared page for immediate consequences and site return states. This is the **Essential** bookkeeping level. Move to Standard or Full Audit only when the campaign actually needs separate claims, evidence chains, faction knowledge, or historical site records. See **CAMP-15** for the three levels.

## Roles and Responsibilities

### The Players

Each player controls one agent. Describe the agent's goal and method rather than naming a rule you hope to trigger. Ask for details that a person in the scene could notice. Make decisions with incomplete certainty, but not without usable information.

Players are responsible for:

- deciding what their agents attempt and why;
- tracking inventory, injuries, Cash, Debt, and personal trackers;
- sharing plans and making space for every player to contribute;
- accepting consequences without surrendering the right to make new choices;
- helping maintain the agreed tone and safety boundaries.

You are not required to protect your agent from every bad outcome. You are responsible for making choices that reveal who the agent is when protection has a price.

### The Warden

The Warden presents the world, portrays its people, tracks active threats, and applies the rules impartially. The Warden is not the author of a predetermined solution and does not compete against the players.

The Warden is responsible for:

- giving enough information for meaningful decisions;
- making danger legible before it becomes irreversible;
- distinguishing impossible actions from risky ones;
- making the immediate danger and likely scale of loss legible before a save;
- allowing success and failure to stand without converting one into the other;
- following consequences from position, time, resources, and established causes;
- making factions pursue recognizable interests rather than arbitrary punishment.

The Warden may conceal identities, motives, routes, and future consequences. The Warden should not conceal essential physical facts that the agents could readily perceive or that their backgrounds would reveal.

### The Whole Table

Everyone shares responsibility for pace, clarity, and safety. When the fiction becomes confusing, pause and restate the immediate situation: where everyone is, what is happening now, what threatens to happen next, and which choices remain available.

Rules disagreements should be resolved quickly during play. Make a temporary ruling, record the question, and verify it after the session. Preserving informed choice matters more than winning an argument over wording.

## The Mission Cycle
<!-- UTRULE: OVER-01 | RULE | Mission cycle -->

Most sessions move through a recurring cycle. Not every phase needs equal time, and a session may begin or end in the middle of one.

### 1. Assignment, Lead, or Declared Objective

The team may receive an authorized mission, follow a personal lead, respond to an emergency, return to a known site, or declare an operation of its own. No sponsor is required. Clarify the concrete objective, current source of information, time pressure if any, known hazards, funding, likely claims on recovered material, and who benefits from success.

### 2. Preparation

The agents gather information, choose equipment, call contacts, arrange transport, establish access, and decide who pays. For a player-led expedition, they also record sponsorship or self-funding, custody terms, and an extraction plan. Preparation can expose false assumptions, create safer approaches, or reveal that the initial objective is incomplete.

### 3. Incursion

The team enters a temporal zone or confronts an unstable relic. Time, terrain, and history create changing conditions. Exploration consumes time and resources. Violence is fast and dangerous. Contamination can outlast the mission.

### 4. Recovery and Choice

The agents locate the target, learn what the briefing omitted, and decide what recovery actually means. A relic can be registered, hidden, sold, destroyed, or used. A rescued person may refuse extraction. Evidence may implicate the faction that ordered the mission.

### 5. Extraction and Debriefing

Leaving the zone does not end the problem. The agents must decide what they report, what they conceal, who receives the recovered material, and how they explain losses, witnesses, and unauthorized activity.

### 6. Weekly Settlement and Downtime

Mission earnings, tier pay, side-income, required expenses, the weekly event, purchases, voluntary Debt payments, and interest are resolved in order. Side-income reserves one or both of the agent's two downtime actions; any actions left are then spent on treatment, investigation, relationships, recovery, or faction obligations.

This cycle is not a script. It is a pressure structure. Players may reject assignments, abandon objectives, negotiate with adversaries, or create their own operations. The consequences should follow what they actually do.

## The Seven Pressures

The character sheet tracks seven forms of pressure. They are related, but none replaces another.

| Tracker | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Cash** | What can the agent pay for right now? |
| **Debt** | How much future income and freedom are already claimed? |
| **Loyalty** | How much does the Division trust, support, and grant access to this agent? |
| **Corruption** | Which active claims does Zhou's network hold, and how strong are they? |
| **Exposure** | What active evidence connects the agent to unauthorized activity? |
| **Temporal Quirks** | What permanent damage has time written into the agent? |
| **Tier** | What institutional standing, pay, and responsibility has the agent earned? |

Trackers describe relationships, evidence, resources, and damage. They are not moral alignment.

High Loyalty does not prove virtue. An agent may be trusted because they are obedient to a harmful institution. Corruption does not measure supernatural evil. It totals active claims based on favors, secrets, dependencies, incriminating evidence, and enforceable promises. Cash does not erase Debt automatically. Exposure does not mean guilt; it totals active evidence and promises that someone will eventually act on it.

Whenever Loyalty, Corruption, or Exposure changes, record the fact behind the number. "Corruption 2" is less useful than "Corruption 2: Zhou holds a serious claim because she paid for Mara's unregistered treatment and possesses the clinic footage." The number tells the table how much pressure exists. The ledger tells the Warden what scene that pressure can create.

## Principles for Players
<!-- UTRULE: PRIN-01 | PRINCIPLE | Player agency -->

### Ask Before You Commit

The Warden should provide enough information to make a meaningful choice, but players must actively investigate. Ask what is visible, what seems unstable, what your background recognizes, how long an action will take, and what could interrupt it. A precise question can reveal a safer method or a more valuable risk.

### State Goal and Method

Say both what you want and how you attempt it. “I get inside” gives the table little to work with. “I use my Division badge to keep the receptionist talking while Reyes crosses the staff corridor” establishes an approach, tools, vulnerabilities, and possible consequences.

### Change the Situation Before Rolling

Good plans matter. Create leverage, secure an exit, isolate a threat, obtain the correct tool, recruit help, or wait for a better moment. Advantage and disadvantage exist, but the most important effect of preparation is often that it removes a save, reduces exposure to danger, or makes an impossible action possible.

### Treat Combat as a Consequence

Attacks normally hit. Armor and HP buy time; they do not make violence safe. Seek cover, create distance, control access, negotiate, distract, ambush, or retreat. Fight when the objective justifies what injury, ammunition, contamination, and attention may follow.

### Treat Inventory as Intent

Ten slots force priorities. Medicine, ammunition, tools, protection, evidence, and relics compete for space. Carrying an item declares that you expect it to matter. Leaving an item behind creates a vulnerability the table has knowingly accepted.

### Remember That Extraction Is Not Resolution

A recovered relic still needs a destination. A rescued witness still has motives. A false report creates evidence. An unpaid obligation survives the mission. Consider who learns the truth, who profits, and what the team must do next week because of today's solution.

### Let Consequences Reveal the Agent

The game does not ask players to pursue failure for its own sake. It asks them to make decisions without erasing every cost. Debt, injury, Quirks, compromised relationships, and institutional scrutiny are playable conditions. Respond to them through further choices rather than treating them as reasons the campaign must stop.

## Principles for the Table
<!-- UTRULE: PRIN-02 | PRINCIPLE | Table causality -->

### No Hidden Essential Information

The Warden may hide what a person wants or what lies beyond a sealed door. The Warden should not hide the smoke, vibration, warped light, or expert knowledge that makes a danger recognizable. If a floor is close to collapse, the scene should contain signs. If an opponent is overwhelmingly dangerous, the fiction should communicate that before combat becomes unavoidable.

### Consequences Follow the Situation

A failed save does not automatically cause damage and does not automatically grant partial success. Apply what the method actually exposed: elapsed time, lost position, noise, damaged equipment, attention, separation, injury, or a closed opportunity. The consequence comes from the established place and action, not from a need to keep a scene moving.

### Failure Is Allowed

A failed roll may simply mean that the attempted objective is not achieved. The locked door remains closed. The witness refuses to talk. The agent does not cross the failing span. Record any time, resource, position, or danger already placed at risk, then continue from the unchanged obstacle and the world that has kept moving around it. Another attempt requires a changed method, better tools, new leverage, or acceptance of another cost.

### No Predetermined Solution

Scenarios provide people, places, objectives, resources, and escalating threats. They do not require the players to discover the author's preferred sequence. When the group invents an unexpected approach, determine whether it is possible, identify the risk, and follow the result honestly.

### Pressure Is Real, Not Predetermined

A Recruit receives $800 per week against $900 in required expenses and begins with $300 Cash and $1,200 Debt. Mission pay, side income, voluntary payments, emergencies, and 3% interest determine what happens next. The system creates pressure, but it does not decree that every agent must betray the Division, serve Zhou, or remain poor forever.

### Make Costs Legible

Players do not need perfect knowledge of the future, but they should understand the immediate category of risk. Before an irreversible decision, clarify whether the likely cost concerns time, harm, contamination, evidence, money, faction trust, or someone else's safety. Surprise should come from what the world contains, not from changing the terms after a choice is made.

### Safety Is Part of Preparation

The game may involve body horror, coercive employment, poverty, addiction, firearm violence, bereavement, medical dependency, and institutional abuse. Before play, establish lines, veils, and a method anyone can use to pause, revise, or remove content. Revisit those boundaries when the campaign changes direction.

No fictional consequence is more important than the people at the table. Changing or skipping a scene does not weaken the game. It protects the trust that makes difficult play possible.

## The Promise of Play

Agents can become more capable, connected, and influential, but they remain vulnerable. They may escape Debt, expose a faction, preserve an impossible life, or transform the institutions around them. They may also return with scars that cannot be purchased away.

The rules do not decide which future is correct. They ensure that time, money, evidence, loyalty, and damage remain present when the players decide what future they are willing to create.
